**Right answer for the wrong reason.** Example 1 is the serious case. Rule 13 asks whether "the cost an input can impose is bounded by a stated limit"; a normalization walk over the result is bounded by exactly the limits `Marshal` already runs under, so 13 does not separate the options. Strike it and the row's own remaining rules decide: ground (d), rule 7 (Go callers, `encoding/json` returns one object type) beats rule 3 (the carriage sentence we ourselves wrote). The procedure, honestly applied, chooses uniform; the author chose the split because the ruling queue already had. The split may still be right, but the ground that makes it right is one the guide never states: the cost of a feature should fall on the caller who opted into it (identity carriage is free for everyone; the walk is paid by `Canonical` callers only). Example 3 reaches the right answer citing 8a for a case whose defining word, scheduling, appears in 8b's test and nowhere in 8a's. Example 4 reaches the right answer by treating a maintainer ruling as decisive while the row's headline cites rule 13; the difference matters because the escalation list would flag the 13-versus-authority framing. Example 8c is right and is labeled with a confidence the scale forbids.

## The precedence as a design philosophy

**Where it produces good APIs.** Authority text first is correct for a library that implements a specification; a designer who overrides RFC 8259 or the pinned JSONata page on taste ships a dialect, and examples 4, 8a, and 10 all benefit from being forced to quote before arguing. Determinism (8b) above loudness (8a) is the right call and not the obvious one: an API that returns different values for equal inputs is worse than one that errors, and example 3 is the payoff. The demand that a rule be claimed with a demonstrable case, and that a rule claimed for both sides is neutral, is the single best mechanism in the document; it is what makes example 12 a clean decision instead of three panels of idiom preference. The ground-dependent swap of 7 and 3 at rank 5 is sound: on a public API the caller's formed expectation is external and expensive to change, while the layer's contract is ours and cheap to rewrite before publication.

**Where it produces worse APIs than a different order.** Three places.

Rule 13 at rank 2 as a *preference* rather than a *gate*. Bounded cost is a property an option either has or lacks; ranking it invites citing it for any O(n) cost, which is what example 1 did. Treated as a gate ("an option whose cost no stated limit bounds is inadmissible"), it decides example 4 identically and cannot be borrowed for example 1. As written, any performance argument that can be phrased as "proportional to input" beats every design consideration below it, and every design consideration is below it.

DX at rank 7. The ratified rule says "DX is supreme inside the constraints"; the precedence puts it below 3, 7, and 9, all of which are preferences an arguer can support with a marginal case. Concretely: six panels called the two-arm switch the largest ergonomic cost of the package, and under this order that fact cannot beat a single doc-comment sentence claimed under rule 3. Reversing 5 and 3 for ground (d) questions changes example 1 and nothing else in the twelve, and it is the order a standard-library reviewer uses: the contract is rewritten to serve the caller, not the caller made to serve the contract.

Rule 9 at rank 6 for class and specification questions. For a project whose premise is that two SDKs agree at the observable boundary, "would two independent implementations agree from the text alone" is close to the definition of a finished specification. It belongs immediately below the authority for ground (a) and (c) questions; for ground (d) it can stay where it is. The precedence already varies rank 5 by ground; it should vary this one too. Example 8a shows the cost of not doing so: the deciding rule was 7 and the row admits 9 was satisfied only because "either algorithm" could be pinned, which is a weaker guarantee than the class needs.

One more structural point. Rule 11's consumer definition ("a caller in a project repository identified by a commit, an open issue, or a named SDK integration point") cannot be met by anything before the first release of a new package. So on a greenfield surface rule 11 defers every gap, forever, and the guide's own P1 ("pending is worse than either answer") says why that is bad. Five independent panels producing the same sixty-line sketch is the strongest evidence a pre-release designer ever gets, and the definition rules it out by name.

## The rules and precedents as principles

**Real principles.** Rule 3 (claim exactly your job) is the layering principle and the guide applies it well in example 11. Rule 7 is least astonishment, correctly pinned to a named audience. Rule 8a and 8b are the two halves of "fail correctly": never a quiet wrong value, never two answers for one input. Rule 9 is specification completeness. Rule 6 is good defaults. Rule 13 is a real constraint. P3 (name the standard-library analogue before the answer) is the best entry in the document and is exactly how Go's API review works; P4 (the obvious reading of a call is its meaning) is the principle of least astonishment applied to signatures and belongs in any guide; P5 (every element has an observable job) is API minimalism stated crisply; P9 and P10 are correct numeric-tower and encoder-fidelity principles that generalize to any host with two numeric kinds or a wire boundary.

**One case in principle's clothing.** Rule 4 ("suspect uniformity that couples") exists to defend one non-uniform choice (example 1) and, as a general principle, is backwards: uniformity is the default virtue of a public API, and the guide should have to argue its way *out* of it, not into it. Rules 1 and 2 are facts about this project's authority model, not design principles, and the table admits as much by redirecting them to step 1. Rule 12 is release process. P6's "sixty lines with six decisions" is a description of one integrator's sketch; the principle underneath (the project owns a decision every integrator would otherwise make differently) is real and should be stated without the anecdote. P7, P1, and P2 are loop process, not design. P11 is an open question with a precedent number on it; a guide should not list "whether X is refused or rounded is the open question" as a precedent.

**Missing principles that a strong API design guide contains.**

- *Pay for what you use.* A cost is paid by the caller who chose the feature, never by every caller. This is the actual ground of examples 1 and 3 and the guide uses it twice without naming it.
- *Prefer the reversible option.* When uncertain, choose the option whose reversal is an addition, not a removal (delete `Close` now; add it back if a job appears). This is Go's compatibility philosophy and the real reason examples 5, 7, and 2's deferral are safe. Rule 12 gestures at it as process; it is a design rule.
- *Uniform unless the difference means something to the caller.* The correct replacement for rule 4. Every `(value, present, err)` triple in the package obeys it; the object split violates it and should have to earn the exception.
- *Errors are part of the surface.* Stable codes, typed sentinels, what is and is not stable in a message. The package does this well (`Code`, `Class`, `errors.Is`) and the guide has no rule that would have produced it; 8a covers only whether an error exists.
- *The first call is the common call.* Rule 6 half-states it. A package whose most likely first input (a struct) fails with an engine code has a rule 6 problem regardless of what rule 11 says about consumers.
- *Make invalid states unrepresentable.* P4's "or it does not compile" is the seed. Compile-time checks beat runtime checks; the guide never weighs this, which is why example 2 could defer `Field` handles purely on allocation grounds.
- *Orthogonality.* Prefer few primitives that compose over many conveniences. The surface has `Eval`, `EvalMarshal`, `EvalJSON`, `Materialize`, `Clone`, `Canonical`, `Member`, `Int64`, `Float64`; a guide should ask which of these compose and which merely accumulate.
- *Cost is documented, never hidden.* The package states every bound; nothing in the guide requires it.

## What I would change

1. **Make 13, 8b, and 8a gates; let DX decide among what passes.** Replace step 3's items 2 to 4 and 7 with: "An option that fails rule 13, rule 8b, or rule 8a with a demonstrable case is inadmissible and enters no precedence. Among admissible options, rule 5 decides, with the consumer named under rule 7; rules 3, 7, and 9 break ties among options whose common path is the same length." Re-run example 1 under this; if the split survives, it survives on the pay-for-what-you-use ground in change 3, and the row says so.

2. **Rank rule 9 by ground.** Amend step 3, item 1: "For a ground (a) or (c) question, rule 9 ranks immediately below the authority text: an option under which two independent implementations could disagree from the text alone loses to one under which they cannot."

3. **Replace rule 4 and add two rules.** Strike rule 4. Add: "14. Uniform unless meaningful. A surface presents one shape for one concept; an option that presents two shapes for one concept must name the difference a caller acts on, with a demonstrable case. 15. Pay for what you use. A cost is borne by the caller who chose the feature; an option that charges every caller for a feature few use loses to one that charges the callers who use it." Rank 14 with rule 7 and 15 with rule 5.

4. **Reconcile 8b with P9, and re-decide 8b.** Amend rule 8b's test: "Within one kind of value, as the artifact's value model declares kinds (a declaration of kinds is ground (a)) ..." and drop the `100` versus `100.0` example or state it as the cross-kind case it is. Then either re-run example 8b under the amended rule and let 8b (rank 3) decide it, or keep the flag and say in the row that the flag exists because P9 is unratified, which is the actual reason.

5. **Fix the escalation contradiction and the confidence labels.** Either strike the bullet "A bound or security property traded against fidelity to an authority" (example 4 shows the procedure can decide it via a maintainer ruling citation) or flag example 4; the document cannot hold both. Relabel 8c `inferred` per the scale, and cite 8b in example 3.

## What I would keep

The demonstrable-case requirement and the neutrality rule ("a rule claimed with such a case for both options is neutral and enters no precedence"); it is what separates a decision from a preference and it produced the two cleanest rows, 11 and 12. The step 1 ladder (normative prose, then examples, then an incorporated authority read as an algorithm with the host's value model substituted, then the reference), which is the right way to read a specification and is the correct answer to the loop's ruling 6g. The "silence plus a working reference is not a design opportunity" sentence, fenced for value, surface, and bound questions. The ground (a) tie-break: if you cannot say in one sentence, quoting the artifact, why a question is not doctrine, it is doctrine. P3, P4, P5, P9, and P10 verbatim. The record format, especially the mandatory "strongest argument against" and the dependency labels, and the batch review sorted by confidence ascending. The flag-not-stop model: keep the current answer, mark it, continue. The confidence scale as written (the labels are right; only their application slipped). Ten of the twelve resolutions, on the merits; the fault lies in how two of them were reached, not in what they concluded.
