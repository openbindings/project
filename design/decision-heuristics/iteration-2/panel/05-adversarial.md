# Guidance panel, iteration 2: the adversarial reader

Cold read of draft 2, 2026-09-19.

## Grades

| Criterion | Grade | One-line justification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Decidability | C+ | The cascade produces an answer, but every load-bearing predicate (demonstrable case, consumer, quoted contract, quoted audience, silence, kind) is satisfiable by text the loop itself writes, so two cold readers reach two answers and both are "by the procedure". |
| Internal consistency | C- | Step 4 says a proposed rule deciding gives `inferred`; examples 1 and 4 are decided by proposed rule 13 at `medium` and `high`. "What escalates" flags a bound traded against authority fidelity; example 4 does exactly that and is decided. Example 7 contradicts P5's own exception clause. Examples 8a, 8c, and 10 pin class algorithms that bind the unwritten TS member, which bullet 3 flags. |
| Generality | C | Step 1.2, P3, P8 through P11, and the whole bound/value/surface trichotomy presuppose a reference implementation and a value model; on a binding specification or interface contract the procedure collapses to step 2 with rule 7's consumer being "consumers of the artifact", which is everyone. |
| Escalation boundary | C | The two hard stops are right. The flags are simultaneously over-inclusive (any question can be phrased as doctrine, any value question binds the other SDK) and under-inclusive (P7 lets the loop rewrite doctrine as "catching up"; step 1.2 adopts behavior with "not a divergence" stamped on it so it never reaches the batch). |
| Clarity and tone | B- | Terms are defined and the register is right; but "lexicographic" plus "each side names one deciding rule" is a puzzle, "(Method.)" is applied inconsistently, and P11 is listed as a precedent while saying its content is the open question. |
| Overall | C+ | A good skeleton whose joints are all authored by the party being constrained; it will let a motivated loop decide anything at `high` and tell the maintainer not to read that row. |

## How to game it

**Step 0 tie-break ("if the loop cannot state in one sentence, quoting the artifact, why not (a), it is (a)").** Both directions are free. To avoid escalation: quote any sentence naming the element ("Eval, Select, and Complete return (value, present, err)") and say "this changes the Go member's signature only". To force escalation and dodge responsibility: say "the TS member would adopt this" and it is (a). The tie-break's real test, "the ground whose text would change if the answer changed", is decided by which text the loop chooses to edit. Example 8a shows the move: `$round` was written into the Go doc (ground d) but the answer is "pinned as a class algorithm", which by the Terms definition of "doctrine that governs more than one artifact of a kind" is ground (a). The loop picked the file, so it picked the ground.

**Step 1 "quote the text" and the ladder.** Quotation without a rule for what counts as silence is selective quotation with a citation. Any authority is silent one level below the question it answers: the pinned page says "half to even", the loop asked "half to even on which value", declared silence, and dropped to step 2 where rule 7 was waiting. The same move works on any rule in any spec: narrow the question until the text stops talking, then decide at step 2. Conversely, "if the authority answers once, that is the answer" lets a generous reading close a question the text does not actually reach. "Read every section the rule cites": the rule cites what the loop wrote.

**Step 1.2 (adopt the reference).** Three exploits. First, the fence "neither a value, a surface, nor a bound is implicated" is vacuous: every "function's result" and "operator's meaning" changes which value results, which is the definition of a value question, so 1.2 either never fires or fires whenever the loop asserts non-implication. Second, the smuggle: the reference implements things the documentation does not describe (integer-key hoisting, 15-significant-digit float rendering, `$number(".5")` rejection, the `${name}` non-recognition). Under 1.2 these arrive as "syntax accepted" or "an operator's meaning", are adopted, and the text forbids calling them a divergence, so they never enter the ledger and the batch never sees them. That is the exact path by which JavaScript artifacts re-enter a Go member with a procedural blessing. Third, "a reference implementation exists" and "named where it is used": jsonata-js at the pinned commit, jsonata-js 2.1.1, the Step Functions dialect, and the old jsonata Go port are all candidates, and the loop names the one that agrees with it.

**Demonstrable case ("a law stated in the artifact or an authority before the finding").** The loop writes the artifact. Iteration N adds a sentence under a D-label; iteration N+1 finds it violated; the law predates the finding. Nothing requires the law to predate the *question*, or to have existed at the commit the loop started from. "A wrong example" is wrong by a standard the loop supplies. "A contradiction between two sentences" can be manufactured by adding the second sentence. Every preference converts to a case in two iterations.

**Consumer (Terms).** "An open issue on that repository" is trivially manufacturable, and the twelve rulings are now issues #1 through #12, so every one of the twelve questions has "an open issue" and the deferral in example 5 is already void by the document's own definition. "A named SDK integration point that would use the element on landing" is a sentence in the record with a future-tense verb. A loop that wants the protobuf Resolver names `openbindings-go/formats/grpc` as the point and P6 fires; a loop that does not want it says "five reviewer sketches are evidence, not consumers". Separately, rule 5 says "the consumer named under rule 7", and rule 7's consumer is a declared audience (a class of caller), not a commit; rule 11's consumer is a commit. The same word has two definitions and rule 5 points at the wrong one.

**Rule 7 "named first, from the surface's own declared audience, quoted".** The Go package's only "callers" are incidental prose ("Callers must inspect present", "callers handing a"); the class README's "Authors" is one sentence about the portable core. Every Go doc says "callers" and every spec says "consumers", so the quote requirement is met by every document ever written. Worse, "the surface" has no fixed granularity: the twelve examples name "the package's callers" (1), "transform authors" (8a), "callers of `Canonical`" (9), "callers of `Unmarshal`" (10), and "Go callers" (12), five audiences for one package, each chosen per question. For `$round`, "Go callers" expect `math.RoundToEven` on the binary value and "transform authors" expect decimal digits; the loop picked the audience that gave its answer, and the "void if you change the consumer" clause only bites the second naming, never the first.

**Rule 8a "under correct use of the surface's own contract".** Circular: each option is tested against the contract that option would write. Example 3 declares the single-goroutine race "reachable only by ignoring its contract" because the option's contract says single-goroutine. Any quiet failure is converted into a contract violation by adding one sentence to the doc. And example 12 applies the test inconsistently: the doc says "Callers must inspect present", so `v, _, err` is ignoring the contract and is rule 7's to weigh, not an 8a case; by the document's own sentence, 8a is not neutral in example 12.

**Rule 8b plus P9.** P9 lets "kind" be defined by representation, then says 8b's spelling and grouping tests apply "within a kind". Shrink kinds until every input is its own kind and 8b never fires. Example 8b does this openly: "three error classes for one product under grouping, though P9 makes that within-kind and neutral". A precedent (by step 4's own scheme, `inferred` grade material) is used to neutralize a ratified rule ranked third. "Ranks with rule 8b" for a precedent whose effect is to switch rule 8b off is not a rank, it is a veto.

**Rule 13 (proposed).** Ranked second, above every ratified rule but 1 and 2, while proposed; and the confidence rule in step 4 (proposed rule decides → `inferred`) is violated by both examples that use it. Its test, "bounded by a stated limit", is failed by example 1's own wording: an O(result) walk is "proportional to input", which is linear, which `MaxWork` (charged "per byte or per value it visits, including values that are carried") already bounds. So rule 13 was claimed with a false case and won at rank 2 over rule 7. The general exploit: dress any surface or ergonomic preference as a cost ("uniformity costs a walk", "a sentinel costs an allocation") and jump the queue. "Whatever the reference does" also collides with step 3 ranking rules 1 and 2 above 13: if the authority text answers and the loop wants otherwise, call it a bound question ("always a step 2 question") and step 1 never runs. Example 4 does this: the rulings record says two reviewers believe the pinned page names JavaScript syntax and asks the loop to "verify the pinned page before ruling"; the example says "whatever the pinned page says" and never verified.

**Rule 3 "with the layer's job quoted from the layer's own contract".** The loop wrote the contract. "A job the arguer describes rather than quotes does not count" only shifts the description one iteration earlier.

**Rule 9 "given the option's text alone".** Satisfied by pinning anything; example 8a says so ("rule 9 is satisfied by pinning either algorithm"). Vacuous for every question where the loop writes the pin, which is all of them.

**Step 3 "each side names one deciding rule" and "claimed for both is neutral".** Neutrality only happens when both sides honestly claim the same rule. Both sides are the same author. If A claims 8a and B's honest claim is also 8a, B claims 13 or 8b instead and lexicographic order decides for B. The mechanism relies on the arguer conceding the strongest framing to the opponent.

**"Pick the option with the shorter text."** The loop writes both texts.

**Re-run trigger ("a lens that has not raised the record's counterargument raises it").** The record's two-line counterargument is authored by the loop. Write it narrowly and every reformulation is new; write it broadly and nothing is. And "unstable: pinned at its first answer" rewards deciding fast and badly, since the first wrong answer becomes the sticky one.

**Batch review: "silence is Keep" and "only the `inferred` rows and the flagged items need reading closely".** Combined with the confidence inflation above, the maintainer is told not to read the rows most likely to be wrong. A loop that wants a decision to land marks it `high`, lists no dependents, and it lands on silence. A loop that wants a decision protected lists many dependents so a Reverse looks expensive; the cost signal is authored by the party under review.

**Published.** pkg.go.dev lists `main` pseudo-versions without a release; openbindings.com renders pages from the web repo's `main`; the definition says the first is not published and the second is. A loop can argue that a Go API on `main` is unpublished (so it decides freely) and that a docs page is published (so it flags to dodge). Both readings are inside the text.

**"Changes what another repository, the other SDK, or a member not yet written must do at an observable boundary."** In a two-SDK project with a conformance suite, every value question satisfies this, so the bullet is either always on (examples 8a, 8c, 10 all pin class algorithms and should all flag) or never on (the loop says "the member is not yet written, so nothing must do anything yet"). The document takes both positions: 6 flags for binding members, 8a decides while binding them.

**Maintainer ruling and "the kind it was stated for".** The kind is inferred by the loop. Example 8b honestly flags the inference; example 4 makes the opposite move ("applies to an instance of its stated kind, a citation") for the same README's fidelity-over-coverage rule. Nothing in the text says which of those two is the correct reading of "kind", so the loop chooses per question.

**P7.** The procedure retroactively invalidates every maintainer ruling not re-derived under it ("an entry that predates the procedure has not passed it"), then rewrites the doctrine "to state the grounds actually used". It is marked ground (a), so it flags; but a flagged rewrite carries the recommendation and a "marked hole", and a maintainer who reads only `inferred` rows will meet the rewrite as a fait accompli in the artifact. This is "implementation is never authority" inverted: the loop's practice becomes the doctrine's ground with a procedural name.

**P11.** Listed as a precedent, cited as evidence, and its text says its content is the open question. A precedent that says "pending" is a slot for whichever answer the citing loop wants.

**Stopping condition ("a flagged item counts as pinned for a loop's blocked condition").** Flag everything and the loop is blocked by procedure; flag nothing and it never is. Both escalation directions are available to a loop under pressure to stop or to keep moving.

## Three resolutions reversed

**Example 4, regex dialect: reversed to the JavaScript dialect (or a flag).** Step 1 comes first and outranks rule 13. The rulings record states that two lenses believe the pinned documentation's regular-expression page names JavaScript syntax and asks the loop to verify before ruling; the example never did. Under the Terms, that page is an incorporated authority, and step 1.1 reads it "as an algorithm over the abstract value with the host's value model replaced by this artifact's": the dialect is the algorithm, the string type is the value model. The dialect is a surface and value question (which syntax is accepted, what `$match` returns), not a bound question; the bound question is separately answered and already was: the Go doc says "a regex operation is charged the subject's length and the program's size as work", and a backtracking engine that charges matching steps against `MaxWork` is bounded by a stated limit. So rule 13 is satisfied by both options and is neutral; step 1 decides for the incorporated dialect; rule 7 (audience: "Authors", quoted from the README) confirms it, since authors write `(?<=\$)\d+` against the playground. If the loop insists the safety property matters more than fidelity, the fourth escalation bullet is written for exactly this ("a bound or security property traded against fidelity to an authority") and the item flags with RE2 as the recommendation and the stub keeping its current text. Either way, `high` and decided is not an outcome the document's own rules produce. Can the document tell these apart? No: the difference is whether "charged per step" counts as "bounded by a stated limit", and rule 13's wording says it does.

**Example 7, `Close`: reversed to keep it.** P5's own last sentence: "A method whose job is to end something (a cancel function, a borrow window) has one." The quoted contract names that job twice: "while an Evaluation is in use, which ends when Close returns" and "Values returned by an Evaluation's Select or Complete are shared with the Evaluation until Close and must not be modified before then." Rule 3 with the contract quoted: `Close` claims exactly the job the contract names. Rule 8a: delete `Close` and the "until" has no end, so every selected value is shared with the memo for the Evaluation's whole lifetime; a caller who mutates a result after its last `Select` and then calls `Complete` corrupts the memoized field with no error, under correct use of a contract that no longer says when the borrow ends. P3 by structure: a per-input handle (`sql.Rows`, the example's own analogue for D-3) has `Close`; `json.Decoder` does not; two analogues disagree and decide nothing, so P3 is neutral. Rule 7: Go callers expect `defer ev.Close()` on a handle returned from a `Prepare`. Keep, at `high`. Can the document tell these apart? No: both sides quote the same borrow sentence, one as proof that `Close` guards nothing, the other as the sentence that names its job, and P5 contains both readings.

**Example 1, result object type: reversed to a uniform result.** Rule 13 is not claimable: the example's own case says the conversion is "a cost proportional to input", and the artifact bounds every per-value cost by `MaxWork` ("charged per byte or per value it visits, including values that are carried") and every result by `MaxOutputNodes`. "Bounded by a stated limit" is met, so rule 13 is unclaimed and the precedence starts at 8b. Rule 8b: one object value produces two Go types depending on how the expression was spelled (`$` versus `{ "a": $.a }`, a grouping-of-the-expression difference for an identical value), and the example's own answer concedes that "every consumer pays the two-arm switch". Rule 8a: under correct use, `m, _ := out.(map[string]any)` yields a nil map with no error for every constructed object; that is the same quiet path the document accepts as a demonstrable case in example 12. Rule 7: the package's callers, quoted, expect what `encoding/json` does, one object type. Uniform `*Object` at `medium`, with carriage-by-identity for maps retired as the cost of one type. Can the document tell these apart? No: it turns on whether a linear cost under an existing limit is "unbounded", and the text of rule 13 says it is not.

## Defenses present and missing

**Present, and worth keeping.** The two hard stops. The quote-do-not-paraphrase rule. "A law first stated in the finding is a preference." The void-on-consumer-change clause. P2's "convergence detects facts, not taste". The requirement to record the strongest argument against and the option not taken verbatim. The depends-on graph and Reverse-reopens. The default-to-(a) tie-break, which is over-inclusive but errs in the safe direction. The batch sorted by confidence ascending.

**Missing.**

1. Nothing stops the loop from citing text it wrote. Add: *"A law, contract sentence, or audience quoted in a step 1 or step 2 claim must exist in the artifact at the commit the loop's current iteration started from, and the record cites that commit. Text added by this loop under a `D-` label is loop precedent, not a law, whatever iteration added it."*

2. Confidence is not policed. Add: *"A decision whose deciding rule is numbered above 12, or is a `P-` precedent, is recorded `inferred` regardless of the strength of its case. A decision depending on a flagged item is recorded `inferred` and marked `depends on flagged`."* Examples 1, 4, 7 (depends on 3, fine), 8c (depends on flagged 8b), and 10 (P8) are re-marked.

3. Silence is undefined. Add: *"An authority is silent only when the loop quotes the passage nearest the question and shows that both options are expressible in the authority's own vocabulary and neither is chosen. A question the authority's vocabulary cannot express is not silence; it is a value-model question at ground (a) or (c)."*

4. The consumer is chosen per question. Add: *"The artifact names its rule 7 audience once, in its header, in one sentence. Every rule 5, rule 7, P3, and P10 claim in that artifact uses that audience. Naming a narrower or different audience for one question is the void move."*

5. Rule 11's consumer is manufacturable by filing an issue. Add: *"An issue counts as a consumer only when it is filed by someone other than the loop and names the calling code. An issue the loop filed to record a ruling is not a consumer."*

6. Step 1.2 adoptions are invisible. Add: *"A behavior adopted under step 1.2 is recorded as a `D-` with ground `step 1.2, <reference>@<commit>` and listed in the artifact's ledger under `reference-adopted`. 'Not a divergence' governs the ledger class, never whether the row exists. A step 1.2 adoption that the reference's own documentation contradicts is a step 1.1 question and not eligible."*

7. Rule 13's test is met by any existing limit and its "whatever the reference does" collides with step 3. Add: *"A cost the artifact's existing limits already bound is not a rule 13 case. Rule 13 is claimed with the input, the cost, and the limit that fails to bound it. Where an authority's text answers and rule 13 would override it, the question is the fourth escalation bullet and flags."*

8. The "other SDK / member not yet written" bullet is always-on or never-on. Replace with: *"A decision that adds, removes, or changes the expected value of a class conformance fixture, or that the class document must state for a second member to agree, is flagged. A decision the Go member could reverse without a second member noticing is not."*

9. "Silence is Keep" reaches rows that should not land by default. Add: *"Silence is Keep for `high` rows only. A `medium` or `inferred` row without a mark, and every flagged row, carries into the next batch unlanded."*

10. Nothing binds the two-line counterargument to be the strongest. Add: *"The strongest argument against is written by a lens other than the one that argued for the answer, or it is the argument a prior panel actually made, quoted."*

## What I would change

1. **Police confidence and rank rule 13 as what it is.** Replace step 4's confidence paragraph with: *"`high`: a ratified rule (1 through 12) or a step 1 text decides it outright. `medium`: step 3 precedence among ratified rules decides it. `inferred`: any decision whose deciding rule is numbered above 12 or is a `P-` precedent, or whose authority was unread, or which depends on a flagged item."* And move rule 13 in step 3 to *"between rule 8a and rule 7, until ratified"*. Re-mark examples 1, 4, 8c, and 10 accordingly. This alone closes the biggest hole: a proposed rule currently decides two of the twelve at rank 2 and hides them from the batch.

2. **Freeze what may be quoted.** Insert into step 1 and rule 3, 7, and 8a: *"Text quoted as a law, a contract, or an audience must exist at the commit the iteration began from; the record cites the commit. Text added under a `D-` label is loop precedent."* And in Terms, under Demonstrable case: *"A law must predate the question, not only the finding."*

3. **One audience per artifact.** Add to Terms: *"**Audience.** The class of caller the artifact names once, in its header. Rule 5, rule 7, P3, and P10 use the audience and nothing narrower."* Then repair example 8a: with "Go package callers" as the audience, 8a's rule 7 claim fails and the row becomes a flag under bullet 3 (it pins a class algorithm), which is where it belongs.

4. **Make the escalation bullets testable and reconcile the examples with them.** Replace bullet 3 with the conformance-fixture test in item 8 above, and add to bullet 4: *"Rule 13 never overrides a step 1 answer; where it would, the item flags with rule 13's case as the recommendation."* Then re-run example 4 under those words and record it as flagged, with RE2 as the recommendation. The document currently says one thing in "What escalates" and does the other in the table, and a loop will cite the table.

5. **Give step 1.2 a paper trail and define silence.** Add the `reference-adopted` ledger sentence from item 6 above to step 1.2, and the silence definition from item 3 to the top of step 1. Without these, the reference's JavaScript artifacts can enter any member with "not a divergence" written on them, and any spec sentence can be declared silent by asking one level down.
